Why Pros Are Ditching Hybrids, and Why You Shouldn't

Hybrid use in the PGA Tour top 100 fell from 32% to 13%. On the LPGA it's 70%. The 15 mph gap explains both, and one man won a major with one.

BY GOLFRAW EDITORIAL · ON SITE, OAKMONT PA PUBLISHED FRI 13 JUN 2026 · 07:30 UPDATED 09:42 9 MIN READ

Key Takeaways

  • The PGA Tour Exodus: Only 13 of the top 100 PGA Tour players regularly bag a hybrid, down drastically from 2010.
  • Speed Dictates Gear: At 102+ mph clubhead speeds, hybrids produce severe hooks. At 87 mph (average LPGA and amateur), they produce high, straight ball flights.
  • The Replacements: Elite pros are replacing hybrids with 7-woods, 9-woods, Callaway Apex UWs, and Utility Irons.
  • Amateur Data: Arccos data analyzing over 200 million shots proves hybrids beat 4-irons in GIR percentages for every amateur bracket except scratch golfers.

How Far Hybrid Usage Has Fallen on the PGA Tour

If you attended a PGA Tour event in 2010, you would regularly see 130 to 140 hybrids in the field. Today, they are a dying breed on the men's circuit. Current equipment audits reveal that only 13 of the top 100 players in the world consistently bag a hybrid. The usage rate has plummeted from roughly 40% a decade ago down to just 13%.

What Is Replacing Them?

Nature abhors a vacuum, and the 225-yard gap in a PGA Tour bag has been filled by three distinct clubs:

  • High-Lofted Fairway Woods (7-woods, 9-woods): Players like Ludvig Åberg, Patrick Cantlay, Max Homa, and Tyrrell Hatton have fully embraced the 7-wood. Tommy Fleetwood and Adam Scott occasionally deploy 9-woods. These clubs launch higher, spin more, and land softer than hybrids.
  • The Hybrid-Wood Tweener: The Callaway Apex UW is heavily favored by Xander Schauffele and Akshay Bhatia. It offers wood-like launch with hybrid-like versatility without the severe draw bias.
  • Utility Irons: Gary Woodland and Min Woo Lee prefer driving irons for piercing ball flights in windy conditions, relying on their immense swing speeds to generate adequate launch.

Why Pros Moved Away: The High-Speed Hook

The death of the hybrid on the PGA Tour comes down to clubhead speed and center of gravity. Hybrids feature deep, low, and heel-biased weighting designed to help amateurs launch the ball and fight slices. When a tour pro swings a hybrid at 102 mph, that same weighting causes the clubface to snap shut, producing a violent, uncontrollable hook.

Furthermore, hybrids lack workability. Xander Schauffele famously swapped out his equipment ahead of his Open Championship victory at Royal Troon, opting for clubs he could reliably fade—a shot shape that is notoriously difficult to execute with a modern hybrid at tour speeds.

What the 200M Shot Amateur Data Proves

While the PGA Tour avoids them, amateurs absolutely shouldn't. Arccos Golf and Shot Scope data analyzing over 200 million golf shots reveals a brutal truth: unless you are a scratch golfer, you should not be hitting a 4-iron. Across all mid-to-high handicap brackets (5-20), hybrids unequivocally outperform long irons in both total distance and Greens in Regulation (GIR) percentages. The 15 mph swing speed gap between a Tour pro and a 12-handicap perfectly utilizes the hybrid's heel-weighting to straighten out natural slices and elevate the ball.

Rules Audit: Rule 4.1b (The 14-Club Limit)

When swapping between long irons, hybrids, and 7-woods to dial in yardages, players must rigidly adhere to Rule 4.1b. A player must not start a round with more than 14 clubs. The penalty for exceeding this limit is severe: two penalty strokes for each hole where a breach occurred, with a maximum penalty of four strokes per round.

Fact-Checking 5 Common Equipment Myths

  • Myth 1: No one wins majors with hybrids anymore. False. Aaron Rai won the 2026 PGA Championship at Aronimink bagging a Titleist GT2 24° hybrid.
  • Myth 2: 7-woods are only for seniors. False. The hardest swingers on the PGA Tour (like Cantlay and Åberg) use them to stop the ball on firm greens.
  • Myth 3: Scratch golfers shouldn't use hybrids. False. While the cutoff for hybrid advantage peaks around scratch, many + handicaps successfully bag them; Russell Henley won the Charles Schwab Challenge using one.
  • Myth 4: A 3-iron and a 3-hybrid go the same distance. False. Even with identical lofts, the longer shaft and hotter face of a hybrid typically produce 10-15 more yards.
  • Myth 5: Hybrids are hook machines for everyone. False. They are hook machines at 100+ mph; at 85 mph, they are slice-correcting lifesavers.

The Raw Verdict

Don't let PGA Tour trends ruin your scorecard. The 7-wood is a brilliant club, but for the average amateur swinging 85 mph, the hybrid remains the ultimate cheat code for long approach shots.

FAQ

Why do PGA Tour pros hook hybrids?

Hybrids are built with a heel-biased center of gravity to help amateurs fight slices. When swung at tour speeds (100+ mph), this weighting causes the clubface to close rapidly, resulting in severe hooks.

Should a mid-handicap amateur use a 4-iron or a hybrid?

Data shows that mid-to-high handicap amateurs hit hybrids significantly higher, straighter, and with a better Greens in Regulation percentage than a 4-iron.
