2026 Tour Championship Prize Money Payout: $355K Last

Every spot from $10 million down to $355,000, plus the reason two different payout tables are circulating and which one the Tour actually publishes.

By GOLFRAW Editorial Published 27 Aug 2026 Updated 27 Aug 2026, 20:15 CEST 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • The 2026 purse is $40 million, paying $10,000,000 to the winner and $355,000 to 30th.
  • Two tables are circulating. The $355,000 floor is the 2026 figure; the $280,000 one you may have seen belongs to the earlier structure.
  • The purse is not the FedExCup bonus. The roughly $100 million bonus pool is settled before East Lake begins.
  • Scottie Scheffler arrived having already banked $33 million — $10m for the post-regular-season standings plus a $23m FedExCup bonus.
  • The winner's real prize is the five-year exemption, not the cheque.
  • Not published: a complete 30-row official table. The Tour releases anchor positions, and this page prints those rather than filling the gaps with guesses.

Thirty players, $40 million, and nobody misses a cut. The Tour Championship pays every man in the field, which is why the interesting number is not the $10 million at the top but the $355,000 at the bottom — the guaranteed floor for turning up and completing 72 holes at East Lake.

It is also the number two different tables disagree about, which is the reason half the payout charts circulating this week do not match the other half. Here is what the Tour publishes, where the rival figure came from, and which parts of the money have nothing to do with this tournament at all.

The payout, position by position

$10,000,000 to the winner, $355,000 to 30th, out of a $40 million purse. Every player in the field is paid.

One caveat before the table, because it matters more than the numbers in it. The Tour does not publish a complete thirty-row breakdown. It releases anchor positions, and the rest of what you see online is other people interpolating between them. The table below prints the anchors that actually exist. Why two tables exist

The short version: one of them is out of date, and it is out of date in a way that is easy to miss because the top of the table is identical.

The winner's $10 million has not moved. Neither has second, third or the rest of the top ten. The changes are concentrated at the bottom of the field, where the floor rose to $355,000. If you are comparing two charts by glancing at the first few rows, they look like the same document. They diverge exactly where nobody checks.

That is also why the older figure has been so persistent. It was correct when it was published, it was widely syndicated, and nothing about the headline number flagged it as stale.

What the FedExCup winner gets besides the $10 million

A five-year PGA Tour exemption — which for most of this field is worth more than the cheque.

For a player already inside the elite tier, five years of exempt status is a formality. For everyone else it is the single most valuable thing on offer at East Lake. It removes the reshuffle, the sponsor-invite scramble and the annual arithmetic about keeping a card, and it does so for half a decade.

Prize money gets spent or invested. An exemption buys the thing money cannot: time to play badly for a while without consequences.

The $100 million that was already gone

The FedExCup bonus pool is settled before East Lake starts. It is not part of the $40 million purse.

This is the most common misreading of the week, and it inflates every “how much did the winner make” headline you will see on Sunday night. The roughly $100 million season-long bonus pool is distributed on the standings after the BMW Championship, across the top of the FedExCup order. By the time anyone tees off at East Lake, that money has been allocated.

Scottie Scheffler is the clearest illustration. He arrived at the Tour Championship having already banked $33 million across two separate settlements: $10 million for finishing top of the post-regular-season standings, and a $23 million FedExCup season bonus paid out after the BMW. Neither figure is Tour Championship prize money, and neither depends on a single shot he hits this week.

So a player can have the best financial season in the sport's history and still finish 30th at East Lake for $355,000. The two systems are simply not connected.

J.J. Spaun's withdrawal, and what happens to the money

J.J. Spaun withdrew during the first round with a shoulder problem, which raises a question the format does not usually have to answer: what happens to a guaranteed payout when the field is fixed at thirty and there is no cut to fall through?

The general convention on Tour is that a player who starts a tournament and then withdraws is treated as the last finisher for payment purposes rather than forfeiting entirely. Applied here that would mean the bottom-of-field share.

We are not going to state a figure for it. The Tour has not published a line-by-line ruling for this withdrawal, and the difference between the convention and the specific decision is exactly the kind of gap where confident numbers get invented and then repeated. When the payout sheet is published, this section gets updated with the actual number.

Four myths worth killing

What people keep getting wrong

  • Myth 1 — the winner makes $10 million. Fact: the winner makes $10 million in prize money. Any larger figure quoted on Sunday is stacking the FedExCup bonus on top, and that bonus was decided before the tournament started.
  • Myth 2 — last place gets $280,000. Fact: $355,000. The lower figure is the earlier structure and is the single most-copied stale number of the week.
  • Myth 3 — you have to make the cut. Fact: there is no cut. Thirty players start, thirty players are paid, and everyone begins at even par.
  • Myth 4 — the purse and the bonus pool are one pot. Fact: they are separate systems with separate rules and separate settlement dates. $40 million is the purse. The roughly $100 million bonus pool is not.

The Raw Verdict

Thirty players will be paid at least $355,000 this week for a tournament nobody can be eliminated from, and the man most likely to finish top of the pile already has $33 million in the bank regardless of what he shoots.

That is not a criticism of the players. It is a description of what the FedExCup has become: a season-long bonus scheme with a golf tournament attached to the end of it, where the trophy and the money are decided by two different mechanisms that happen to share a week.

The five-year exemption is the only part of the prize that still behaves like a sporting reward. Everything else was settled by arithmetic in Memphis.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does the 30th-place finisher earn at the 2026 Tour Championship?

$355,000. That is the figure on the table the PGA Tour publishes for 2026, and it is the number this page uses throughout. A widely shared alternative table lists $280,000 for the same position; that figure belongs to the earlier structure and is the single biggest reason two payout tables are circulating this week.

What is the total purse for the 2026 Tour Championship?

$40 million, with $10 million to the winner. That is prize money and is entirely separate from the roughly $100 million FedExCup bonus pool, which is settled before anyone tees off at East Lake.

Does the FedExCup winner get anything besides the money?

Yes, and the part that matters most is not cash. The FedExCup champion receives a five-year PGA Tour exemption, which removes any question of status for half a decade. For a player outside the elite tier that security is worth more than the cheque.

Did Scottie Scheffler already have $33 million before the tournament started?

Yes, from two separate settlements: $10 million for topping the post-regular-season standings and $23 million as the FedExCup season bonus paid after the BMW Championship. Neither is Tour Championship prize money, and both were banked before the first tee shot at East Lake.

What happens to a player's money if they withdraw mid-round?

The Tour Championship has no cut and a 30-man field, so a withdrawal does not simply void the spot. The convention on Tour is that a player who starts and then withdraws is treated as the last finisher for payment purposes, but the Tour has not published a line-by-line ruling for this specific case and this page does not claim one.
