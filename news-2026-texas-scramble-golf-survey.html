Why Golfers No Longer Want to Play in Texas Scrambles

New survey data exposes handicap manipulation, unfair scoring, and the real reasons players are losing interest in the most popular team format in golf.

BY GOLFRAW EDITORIAL PUBLISHED TUE 04 AUG 2026

The Texas Scramble was supposed to be the format that saved amateur golf. A team-based, social way to enjoy the game without the pressure of individual stroke play. But new survey data reveals a growing backlash, with many golfers actively avoiding these events.

Golfshake's 2026 Open Competitions Amateur Events Survey has uncovered a deep divide over handicap integrity, with concerns about manipulation now influencing whether players enter competitions at all. And nowhere is that more apparent than in the Texas Scramble format.

The Trust Problem: Handicap Manipulation Is Killing the Format

The numbers are stark. According to Golfshake's survey of more than 2,500 golfers, almost two-thirds (66%) of single-figure golfers said concerns over handicap manipulation had actually put them off entering competitions. Among golfers with handicaps above 20, that figure dropped to just 39%.

The problem is particularly acute in scramble events. Golfshake's Derek Clements, who has written extensively about the issue, explains: "It seems to have reached epidemic proportions when it comes to fourball scrambles. Cheating has been observed even when the rewards are minor."

The format's very structure makes it vulnerable. In a Texas Scramble, teams select the best shot after each stroke, and handicaps are applied to the team score. But when players can manipulate their individual handicaps by submitting selective scores or deliberately playing poorly in qualifying rounds, the whole system breaks down.

The Low Handicap Exodus

Perhaps the most telling statistic is this: only 9% of single-figure golfers believe they genuinely have a chance of winning handicap competitions. Among golfers playing off more than 20, that figure more than doubled to 19%.

"As a relatively low handicap golfer, when I play in Open pairs competition I do so with absolutely no expectation of either winning or coming anywhere near the prizes," wrote one Golfshake reader. "In my experience, these events are ALWAYS won by high handicappers. I take part purely and simply for the chance to play different courses."

The data backs this up. 78% of single-figure golfers said they don't believe the World Handicap System creates a fair playing field. 70% have witnessed or suspected handicap manipulation, compared with 55% of mid-handicap golfers and 50% of those playing off more than 20.

The Format Itself Is Broken

Even setting aside the cheating concerns, many golfers have simply fallen out of love with the Texas Scramble format itself.

Golf Monthly's Joel Tadman argues it's "not proper golf." The drive quota system — where each player must contribute a certain number of tee shots — can be frustrating. "You can be the best player in the team by a distance but you're still forced to watch your weaker partners hack it around while you stand there with your hands in your pockets," he wrote.

The scoring is often unrealistic. In a Pure Texas Scramble with no handicaps, teams routinely post scores of 15-under par or better. That's not golf as most people know it. Then there's the pace of play. "They take much longer than they should and can't be enjoyed by anyone who actually wants to play golf," said one Facebook commenter.

The Cheating Problem

The cheating in scrambles has become so brazen that even organizers are speaking out. "Scrambles have gotten out of control," wrote MyGolfSpy's John Barba. "A scramble, on the surface, is fun—until organizers and participants make a friendly competition into an event where no shot matters."

Stories abound of teams posting impossible scores, of players miraculously finding their form in scramble events after struggling all season, of handicaps that bear no relation to actual ability.

The R&A has acknowledged the problem, launching a campaign against handicap manipulation in 2025. "It's cheating," said an R&A spokesperson. "We all need to challenge behaviour that lacks integrity."

What Golfers Actually Want

The survey data suggests golfers aren't giving up on competitive golf entirely. They're just becoming more selective. Many are voting with their feet, avoiding events where they feel the odds are stacked against them.

Some clubs are responding by introducing stricter handicap verification for scramble events. Others are moving away from the format altogether, returning to traditional stroke play or Stableford competitions where individual performance matters more.

The Future of the Texas Scramble

The Texas Scramble isn't dead yet. It remains popular for corporate days, charity events and casual society outings where the emphasis is on fun rather than serious competition.

But for club golfers who take their game seriously, the format has lost its appeal. When two-thirds of your best players don't trust the system enough to enter, you have a problem.

The solution may lie in better handicap verification, more transparent scoring, or simply accepting that some formats work better for social golf than for serious competition. But until golfers feel the playing field is level, the Texas Scramble's decline looks set to continue.
