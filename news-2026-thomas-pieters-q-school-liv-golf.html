Thomas Pieters Says He'd Go Back to Q School. He's Already Done It Once.

BY GOLFRAW EDITORIAL PUBLISHED TUE 28 JUL 2026

The Quick Verdict

Thomas Pieters, the Belgian who joined LIV Golf in January 2023, says that if the league folds he'll enter DP World Tour Qualifying School this winter and earn his way back rather than take invitations. He told Today's Golfer at LIV Golf UK: "I don't have an ego. I'll do whatever I have to do to play."

He has no remaining DP World Tour status, having lost his membership at the end of the 2023 season. He said in May he's "definitely never going back to the PGA Tour." So Q School isn't one option among several. It's the only door left.

Here's the part nobody's mentioning: he's been through it before, and he got through all three stages.

Back to where he started

In 2013, fresh out of the University of Illinois — where he'd won the NCAA Division I individual title beating Jordan Spieth and Patrick Cantlay along the way — Pieters went to the European Tour's Qualifying School and came through all three stages to earn his card.

Two years later he won twice in a fortnight. A year after that he finished fourth at the Rio Olympics, and two weeks later won again. Darren Clarke picked him for the 2016 Ryder Cup at Hazeltine, where he took four points from five matches, the best return by any European rookie to that point and the highest individual score of anyone on either team. He won all three of his matches partnering Rory McIlroy, who then told everyone Pieters was his for the next twenty years.

He reached world No. 23 in August 2017. Fourth at the Masters. Six DP World Tour titles.

So the man contemplating Q School at 34 is the same man who came through it at 21, and everything between those two points was supposed to make the second trip unnecessary.

What he actually said

The "no ego" line is the one running in every headline, and it's fine. But it isn't the interesting quote.

This one is:

He said he'd try for invitations but doesn't want a freebie. He wants to earn his way back, because he doesn't want anybody on the DP World Tour saying oh, he's getting his status back. He wants to do it properly. And he thinks there's still golf in him — that he can still compete with the best — but that he's got a lot to prove first, before he gets back into majors.

Read that again with the last three years in mind. He's not describing a qualifying process. He's describing what he expects the reception to be. A player who left for LIV, who lost his membership, coming back through the side door on invites, and knowing exactly what the locker room would make of it.

That's a more honest account of where LIV players stand with their old tour than any amount of press-conference diplomacy.

On the broader thing, he was blunt: if LIV doesn't exist, he has to prove his way back, has to prove he can win and go on to majors, and that's the consequence. Everyone came here for a reason. If it's gone, it's life.

What changed since April

Quite a lot, and it's worth laying out in order because the coverage keeps flattening it.

April: the news broke at LIV Mexico that the Saudi Public Investment Fund would stop funding the league. Pieters described a grim atmosphere among players and, by his own account, checked out while his colleagues refreshed their phones. Around that time he suggested he was ready to retire — that if purses dropped he might go back to the DP World Tour, but that he could just as easily walk away.

May: he ruled out the PGA Tour entirely.

Now, three months on, at JCB with the league back from what Today's Golfer describes as a long hiatus, he's talking about Q School, about earning it properly, about still having golf in him.

That's a man who has moved from maybe I'm done to here is my route back, and nobody seems to have registered the shift.

The other thing driving it: the 2028 Olympics in Los Angeles, at Riviera. He finished fourth in Rio for Belgium and later withdrew his name from consideration for Paris after losing his tour membership. A medal at Riviera is the specific thing he says he's motivated by, and it happens to require exactly the kind of status Q School would restore.

How hard is Q School, actually?

Genuinely brutal. Reporting on his situation describes the DP World Tour route as three stages and 252 holes, cutting a field of well over a thousand down to somewhere around 20 to 25 cards.

I'd treat those specific numbers as approximate — they come from secondary coverage rather than the tour's own published entry documents, and I couldn't verify them against a primary source. The shape is right regardless. It's the hardest week or three in golf, it's full of desperate people, and a former world No. 23 gets no allowances whatsoever.

Worth adding: he's not alone in planning for this. Graeme McDowell has reportedly been planning a DP World Tour return for 2027, and reports have put the number of LIV players preparing exits at around seven. Pieters is a data point in a pattern, not an outlier.

Where LIV actually stands

Chief executive Scott O'Neil is reportedly trying to raise around $350 million in new investment to keep the league operating into 2027. Pieters says the players get small updates from him and not much else.

Martin Kaymer, meanwhile, has put the chances of LIV's $40 million season finale actually happening at roughly five per cent.

None of that is settled. LIV publishes no accounts, and everything about its finances in circulation is reported rather than documented. But it's enough that a six-time DP World Tour winner is publicly discussing Q School, which tells you something about how the players themselves are reading it.

One more detail worth your time

He finished T23 at LIV Golf UK with a fractured rib.

That's in the same interview, mentioned almost in passing, and every aggregation of this story has dropped it. A guy talking about whether he's still got it, doing that, in the week he's talking about it.

FAQ

Who is Thomas Pieters?

A 34-year-old Belgian, six-time DP World Tour winner, 2016 Ryder Cup star at Hazeltine, fourth at the 2016 Olympics and the 2017 Masters. He joined LIV Golf in January 2023.

What did he say about Q School?

That if LIV folds he'll enter it, because he doesn't have an ego and will do whatever he has to do to play.

Why can't he just rejoin the DP World Tour?

He said in May that he's definitely never going back there.

What about invitations?

He said he'd try for them but doesn't want a freebie — he wants to earn it so nobody can say he was handed his status back.

Has he been to Q School before?

Yes. He came through all three stages in 2013 to earn his first European Tour card.

What's motivating him?

He's named the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics at Riviera, and the chance of a medal for Belgium.

GOLFRAW's Take

There's a version of this story that's about LIV falling apart, and it's the version everyone's writing. The better one is smaller. A man who was told at 24 that he'd be world No. 1, who was Rory McIlroy's chosen partner for the next two decades, is at 34 talking about going back to the same qualifying school he passed as a kid — and saying he'd rather do that than take an invite, because he doesn't want anyone muttering about it in the car park. You can think joining LIV was a mistake and still recognise that as the right answer. Most players in his position would be working the phones for exemptions. He's talking about 252 holes and a five per cent chance of a card, at 34, with a broken rib, because he wants it to count when he gets there. One caveat before you take any of this to the bank: the LIV funding situation is reported, not documented, and Pieters has changed his mind once already this year. In April he was ready to retire. Ask him again in October and the answer might be different. That's not a criticism — it's just what happens when your career depends on a decision somebody else hasn't made yet.
