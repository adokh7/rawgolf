Every Rocket Classic Preview Has the Same Blind Spot

BY GOLFRAW EDITORIAL PUBLISHED TUE 28 JUL 2026

The Quick Verdict

The 2026 Rocket Classic runs Thursday 30 July to Sunday 2 August at Detroit Golf Club. The course has been rebuilt. A $16 million restoration by architect Tyler Rae has taken it from a par 72 to a par 70 at 7,328 yards, and the tournament now plays as a composite course, borrowing the first hole of the club's South Course to serve as its par-4 third.

Cameron Young is the favourite at somewhere between +960 and +1200 depending on your book. Aldrich Potgieter defends. Scheffler and McIlroy are off this week.

And essentially every course-fit ranking you'll read is built on data from a golf course that no longer exists.

Start with the number that matters

Detroit Golf Club's reputation is built on scoring. Look at the past winning totals: Finau at 26 under, Lashley 25 under, Fowler 24 under, DeChambeau 23 under, Potgieter 22 under last year. It's the reason every preview for seven years has said the same three things — wide fairways, receptive greens, back the bombers.

All of those totals were shot at par 72.

Two strokes of par per round, over four rounds, is eight shots. That's before you account for anything else. Hold scoring exactly identical, change nothing about how the course plays, and Tony Finau's 26 under becomes 18 under on this year's card. That's my arithmetic from the Tour's own par and past-results pages — a floor, not a forecast.

In practice it'll be more than eight, because you don't remove two par 5s and replace them with par 4s without making the golf harder. Long par 4s are the least forgiving holes in the game. The par 5s were where the bombers made their hay.

So when you see a ranking telling you to target players who hit it a long way because Detroit yields birdies, that's advice derived from a course that got demolished.

What the Tour itself says

Give the PGA Tour's own preview credit here, because it's the most useful analysis I found and it doesn't pretend.

Their read: on a new or renovated course, ball-strikers hold the early edge, because hitting to specific targets and yardages is the skill that got them there. But professionals learn fast, so players who profile as strong putters emerge by the second round and hang around into the weekend. And the line I'd underline — newcomers have exactly as much course history as anyone who's been booking the same hotel for seven years.

They're also the only source I found that mentions the composite routing at all. The South Course's opening hole is now the tournament's third, which makes this officially a composite venue on Tour, and the South is on Rae's list for future work.

What's actually knowable this week

Not much, which is itself worth saying. Here's what survives scrutiny.

Cameron Young is a legitimate favourite. World No. 3, reigning Players champion, runner-up at The Open a fortnight ago. He's also one of the few whose Detroit record still means something in a limited way — he's among the top few in total strokes gained here across the first five runnings. On the old course. But form like that travels regardless of architecture.

Chris Gotterup is in the best form of anyone not named Young. Three wins this season including the John Deere, three straight top 20s, and at The Open he ranked sixth in true strokes gained off the tee and fifth tee-to-green. That last bit matters more than usual: elite ball-striking is exactly what the Tour says transfers to an unfamiliar layout. The catch is that his actual Detroit record is poor — nothing better than T26 in three starts, including a T67. If you believed course history mattered, you'd fade him. This week, I'm not sure it does.

Jackson Koivun is priced third at around +2000 to +2600 after winning the 3M Open on Sunday in his third start as a pro. A quick correction while we're here: CBS's preview calls him a "23-year-old PGA rookie out of Auburn." He's 21. Their own 3M Open coverage says so. Small thing, but if a piece can't get the age of the guy who won on Sunday right, weigh its model projections accordingly.

The field is stronger than usual. Twenty-one of the world's top 50, six inside the top 15, ten major champions. Schauffele, Clark, Si Woo Kim, Cantlay, Henley, Spaun, Griffin, Matsuyama, Spieth, Koepka. The tee sheet moved throughout tournament week, so no exact field size is given here. The cut is the standard top 65 and ties.

The stakes are real. $10 million, 500 FedExCup points, 45 world ranking points, and it's the 33rd of 34 regular-season events. Anyone hovering near the playoff line — Koivun, for instance, sits exactly on it at 70th — has two weeks to sort it out.

Who I'd actually watch

Not a ranking. A short list, built only on things I can defend.

Cameron Young — best player in the field, best form, and the profile the Tour says wins on unfamiliar ground.

Chris Gotterup — the ball-striking numbers are the most relevant statistic available this week, and his bad Detroit history is now much less relevant than it was a year ago.

Patrick Cantlay and J.J. Spaun — both among the strokes-gained leaders here historically, both plot their way round rather than overpower a course. If the rebuild has added difficulty, that's the profile.

Chris Kirk — one of only two players to have made the cut in every one of the first five runnings here, which speaks to a player who understands the property rather than one who bombs it.

Who I'd be careful with: anybody being recommended purely because they hit it a long way. That thesis has no evidence behind it this week. It might still be right. It just isn't supported yet.

Two things worth knowing about the event's future

You'll see this described as the final Rocket Classic. Be careful, because three separate things are being blended.

One: this is the last edition under the current title sponsor. That's well supported. Two: whether the tournament continues depends on a replacement being found, which is unresolved. Three: separately, questions hang over the venue hosting Championship Series events when the Tour's two-track schedule arrives in 2028.

Those are different questions with different answers, and at least one outlet has collapsed all three into "final edition."

What nobody has, including me

The PGA Tour published its own Power Rankings on Monday. I couldn't access the ranked list — only the course-analysis portion. So I haven't reproduced it, reconstructed it, or quietly presented something else as it. If that specific list is what you came for, it's on pgatour.com and you should read it there.

Two other absences worth flagging. Tyler Rae, the architect who rebuilt the course, has not been quoted in a single preview I found — the one person who could actually tell you how it plays. And no player has said anything publicly about the new layout either. For a week where the course is the whole story, that's a lot of silence.

FAQ

When is the 2026 Rocket Classic?
Thursday 30 July to Sunday 2 August, at Detroit Golf Club.

What par is Detroit Golf Club now?
Par 70, 7,328 yards. It was a par 72 before this year's restoration.

What changed?
A $16 million restoration led by Tyler Rae, plus a composite routing that uses the South Course's first hole as the tournament's par-4 third.

Who's the favourite?
Cameron Young, roughly +960 to +1200 depending on the sportsbook.

Are Scheffler and McIlroy playing?
No, both are off this week.

Who's the defending champion?
Aldrich Potgieter, who won a three-man playoff over Chris Kirk and Max Greyserman in 2025. He's around +8000 this year.

Will the winning score be 25 under again?
Unlikely, and don't assume. Those totals came on a par 72. Removing two shots of par costs eight over four rounds before anything else changes.

What's the purse?
$10 million, with 500 FedExCup points and 45 world ranking points to the winner.

Is this the last Rocket Classic?
It's the last under the current title sponsor. Whether the event continues beyond that isn't settled.

GOLFRAW's Take

This is the most honest week of the year to admit you don't know anything, and almost nobody is taking the opportunity. Every model, every course-fit rating, every "he's a great fit for Detroit" line is trained on seven years of a golf course that got bulldozed. That doesn't make the models useless — form is form, and Cameron Young would be the favourite on a car park. But the specific edge people think they have this week, the course-history edge, mostly evaporated the day the diggers arrived. The Tour's own preview makes the point better than any of the picks content does: everyone in the field has the same amount of experience of this golf course, which is none. If you're betting or setting a lineup, that's not a reason to panic. It's a reason to back the best players and ignore anyone selling you a course fit for a course nobody has played.
