The short answer

What is a blow-up hole in golf?

A blow-up hole is one you finish at double bogey or worse: two or more over par. A few of them usually account for a big share of an amateur’s score. Arccos data puts it at about three a round for a 10-handicapper and four for a 15.

The Round Card is a free golf scorecard analyzer. It finds the blow-ups on your card and shows what they cost. If double bogey is your normal hole that day, only triples or worse count, so the list stays short. Add putts and penalties and it tags what went into each one: a penalty, a three-putt, or extra shots to reach the green.

        How the Round Card reads your card

        Blow-ups. A blow-up is a double bogey or worse. If double bogey is your typical hole that day (the middle of your scores against par), only triples or worse count, so the list shows the holes that really got away. The result says which rule it used.

        What they cost. It adds up the blow-ups against par, sets that against your whole round, and shows what you would have shot had each one been a bogey.

        Greens in regulation, without asking. A green is hit in regulation when you are on it with two shots to spare against par. With your putts entered, score minus putts answers it. TheGrint and SwingU work it out the same way. A hole with no putt is left unknown.

        Three things a card can price. Penalty strokes, putts beyond two, and shots beyond one to reach a green you missed. Those are the usual routes to a double bogey. The next-round focus goes to whichever cost you most, if it cost at least three strokes (two over nine holes) and nothing else tied it. If it points at the greens, our three-putt guide is where to start; for turning any priority into range time, read the honest practice guide.

        How sure it is. A strong signal means the numbers behind a finding were entered on at least 90% of the holes. Some evidence means 60% or more, or a finding that has to guess at the cause. Below 60% a stat is not used at all.

        What it will not do. No strokes gained: that needs the distance of every shot. No handicap benchmarks: the published averages disagree too much to judge one round by. And no “tilt” score: TheGrint’s data found golfers play the holes after a blow-up no worse than the holes before it.

        What to write down after a round

        • Your score on every hole. The one thing the card needs.
        • Putts, counting only strokes on the green. Jot them on the card hole by hole; a round of putts is hard to rebuild afterwards.
        • Penalty strokes: one for out of bounds, a lost ball, water or an unplayable lie. The shot you replay is a normal stroke.
        • Skip fairways. Fairway rate barely moves with handicap (about 50% for scratch golfers and 46% at 20 to 25 in Shot Scope’s data), and the tee shots that really cost you show up as penalties.

        Limitations

        • It works from memory and a card. A wrong putt count or a forgotten penalty moves the findings, which is why each one says how much data is behind it.
        • Picked up? Enter the score you wrote down. If you picked up at net double bogey, the real damage was at least that.
        • One round is one round. A single finding can be a bad day; a pattern needs several cards.
        • Fringe putts count as chips here, as on most stats sheets. Counting them as putts makes it look as if you hit more greens.
        • Nine-hole rounds are saved and shown here, but the Tendency Engine and the Coach Report only average full 18-hole rounds.

        Questions

        What stats should I track after a golf round?

        Your score on every hole is the minimum, and it already shows where the round went: which holes, how many blow-ups, which nine. Add putts and penalty strokes and you can see why. Those two, with greens in regulation worked out from them, cover the usual ways a hole turns into a double bogey. Fairways add little for one round.

        Which golf stats matter most for amateurs?

        The ones that explain big numbers. Mark Broadie’s research found the long game explains most of the gap between better and worse amateurs, and that consistent golfers make few blow-ups. Lou Stagner’s analysis of shot data found penalties are the fastest route to a double bogey at every level, with three-putts a growing share as handicaps rise. Putts per round on its own misleads, because hitting more greens means more putts.

        How can I review a round without tracking every shot?

        Write your score, putts and any penalty strokes on the card as you play, then put the card in here afterwards. It takes a minute or two and needs no phone on the course. The Round Card finds the blow-ups, prices penalties, three-putts and extra shots to the green, and picks one thing for next time only when the card clearly points at it.

        How does it work out greens in regulation?

        From score and putts. Regulation is par minus two strokes to reach the green, so on a par 4 you are on in two. If your score minus your putts is two or less, you hit it. Penalty strokes count as strokes. With no putt recorded, the green is left unknown.
