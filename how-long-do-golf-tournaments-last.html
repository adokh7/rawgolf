How Long Do Golf Tournaments Last?

Four days is the default, not the rule. Three-day tours, no-cut fields, weather Mondays, and the 2028 change that stretches one event to two weeks.

By GOLFRAW Editorial Published 30 Aug 2026 Updated 30 Aug 2026, 11:00 CEST 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Four days, 72 holes, Thursday to Sunday is the default on the PGA Tour, the LPGA Tour and at the majors — but it is a convention, not a rule.
  • PGA Tour Champions and LIV Golf play 54 holes over three days, with no cut, so every player plays every round.
  • The cut falls after 36 holes on Friday. The PGA Tour standard is top 65 and ties.
  • No-cut events exist — limited-field signature events and the 30-player Tour Championship pay everyone who tees it up.
  • Weather can add a day. Lightning, rain and darkness push finishes to Monday, and play resumes from the exact spot it stopped.
  • From 2028 the Tour Championship becomes match play across two weeks — by far the longest event on tour.

Four days. That is the answer almost everyone gives, and it is right about most weeks and wrong about the rest. A standard professional tournament is 72 holes played Thursday to Sunday, one round a day. But three-day events are a fixture of two entire tours, weather routinely adds a Monday, and from 2028 one event stretches across two full weeks.

Here is what actually sets the length, tour by tour, and why the four-day assumption breaks more often than you would think.

The short answer

Three days to two weeks. Four days — 72 holes, Thursday through Sunday — is the standard, but 54-hole three-day events are common and the 2028 Tour Championship runs across two weeks.

The standard four-day schedule, hour by hour

A full-field week is 72 holes of stroke play: one 18-hole round on each of Thursday, Friday, Saturday and Sunday. The winner is whoever takes the fewest strokes across all four. There is no qualifying round inside the tournament proper — Monday qualifying happens before the week starts, and a player who gets through it is simply in the field on Thursday like anybody else.

The field goes out in waves. On a 144-player week, players are grouped in threes and sent from the first and tenth tees in a morning wave and an afternoon wave, then the two waves swap on Friday so everybody plays one early round and one late one. That swap matters: morning greens are smoother and afternoon wind is usually stronger, so the draw is a genuine variable rather than an administrative detail.

After the cut the field roughly halves, groups drop from threes to twos, and the weekend rounds go off in a single wave with the leaders last.

The Friday cut

The cut is applied after 36 holes. On the PGA Tour the standard is the top 65 and ties.

Everyone below that line finishes Friday, misses the weekend and earns nothing. It is the single most consequential moment in a normal tournament week, and it is why Friday afternoon leaderboards are watched more closely at the bottom than the top.

Two things complicate the standard. Some limited-field events cut to a smaller number than 65. And a handful of events do not cut at all — the no-cut format is standard in the FedExCup Playoffs finale, where a 30-player field plays all four rounds and every player is paid. If you want the position-by-position detail on what that pays, our Tour Championship payout breakdown covers it.

The 10-shot rule is the one most people still quote and the one most likely to be out of date. It was a secondary provision that let anyone within ten shots of the lead play the weekend even if they sat outside the numerical cut line. It has been dropped in the places it was best known, and the tours have moved toward a single clean numerical line instead. Do not assume it applies at the event you are watching.

The events that are not four days

PGA Tour Champions plays 54 holes over three days with no cut. Every player in the field plays all three rounds. The reasoning is straightforward: a smaller field of over-50 players does not need a cut to manage numbers, and three rounds fits a tighter television window.

LIV Golf also plays 54 holes over three days with no cut — the name is the Roman numeral. It goes further than the Champions Tour by using a shotgun start, sending every group out simultaneously from different holes, which compresses a round into a single block rather than a wave sequence.

The majors are all 72 holes over four days, but they do not share one cut rule. Each championship is run by a different governing body and sets its own line. Assuming a single figure across all four is the most common mistake in this area. Our majors guide goes through them individually.

Why a round takes five hours

Rule 5.6b recommends a player make a stroke in no more than about 40 seconds once it is their turn. Tournament rounds still take four and a half to five and a half hours, because the constraint is the course, not the individual.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Put 144 players out in threesomes and you have 48 groups moving through 18 holes. Every par three is a queue. A group waiting on the tee is not slow — it is stuck behind the group in front, which is stuck behind the one in front of that. Pace collapses backwards down the course from a single slow group, and the field behind absorbs all of it.

The USGA's pace-of-play work aims recreational golfers at something under four hours for a singles round, and the gap between that and a tour Saturday is mostly field size and stakes: professionals get full green-reading time, rulings take minutes, and nobody is picking up.

Rule 5.6b exists precisely because the governing bodies recognise the individual contribution. The 40-second guideline is a recommendation about readiness, not a shot clock, and it applies from the moment it is genuinely your turn to play.

Weather, suspensions and the Monday finish

The most common reason a four-day tournament becomes a five-day tournament is weather.

Lightning clears the golf course immediately — it is a safety call, not a playing-conditions one, and it is not negotiable. Heavy rain stops play when greens will not hold a ball or standing water makes areas unplayable. Darkness ends the day whether or not the round is finished.

When play is suspended, players mark their position and resume from the exact spot. Nothing is replayed. That means a delay does not shorten the tournament, it pushes it: a long Thursday stoppage moves Friday back, which moves the weekend back, and if there is not enough daylight left on Sunday the finish moves to Monday. Tournaments have been reduced to 54 holes when weather made a fourth round impossible, but organisers exhaust every option first, because a shortened tournament is a compromised one.

2028: the finale becomes a two-week event

From 2028 the Tour Championship abandons 72-hole stroke play entirely for a match play postseason spread across two weeks.

As we reported in our breakdown of the 2028 format, week one is a group stage: 32 players in eight four-man round-robin groups, with the top two from each group advancing. Week two is a 16-player single-elimination bracket ending in an 18-hole championship match.

That makes it, by a distance, the longest event on the calendar — and structurally the strangest, because a player can be eliminated having shot better golf than someone who advances. That criticism has come from inside the locker room, not just outside it.

Three things people get wrong

Common misconceptions

  • Myth 1 — all professional tournaments are four days. Fact: two entire tours play 54 holes over three days as their standard, not as an exception.
  • Myth 2 — the cut is always the top 70. Fact: the PGA Tour standard is the top 65 and ties, some events cut to fewer, and the majors each set their own line.
  • Myth 3 — the 10-shot rule still applies. Fact: it has been dropped where it was best known. Check the event rather than assuming.

The Raw Verdict

Four days is a television convention that hardened into an assumption. It is not in the Rules of Golf, it is not shared by every tour, and it survives Thursday to Sunday only when the weather cooperates.

The useful way to think about tournament length is not days but holes and cut. Seventy-two holes with a Friday cut is one kind of week. Fifty-four holes with no cut is a different sport with the same clubs — nobody goes home, so nobody is playing scared on Friday afternoon.

And in 2028 the season ends with a format where the number of days stops being the interesting question at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a golf tournament last?

Four days is the standard on the PGA Tour, the LPGA Tour and at the majors: 72 holes played Thursday through Sunday, one round a day. It is the default rather than a rule. PGA Tour Champions and LIV Golf play 54 holes over three days, and from 2028 the Tour Championship becomes a two-week match play event.

Why do some tournaments only last three days?

Because they play 54 holes instead of 72. PGA Tour Champions and LIV Golf both use three rounds with no cut, which means every player in the field plays every round and the event finishes a day earlier. Fewer holes, not fewer hours per day.

What is the cut and when does it happen?

The cut happens after 36 holes, on Friday evening. On the PGA Tour the standard is the top 65 and ties, and everyone below that line goes home without a cheque. Some events cut to a smaller number, and the no-cut events do not cut at all.

How long does one round of golf take on tour?

Between four and a half and five and a half hours for a threesome on a full-field week, which is well beyond the pace a recreational four-ball is asked to keep. Rule 5.6b recommends a player take no more than about 40 seconds to play once it is their turn, but with 144 players sent out in two waves the bottleneck is the course, not any one player.

Why do tournaments sometimes finish on Monday?

Weather. Lightning clears the course, heavy rain makes greens unplayable, and darkness ends play whether or not the round is done. Suspended play is resumed from the exact spot, so a Thursday delay can push the whole week back and force a Monday finish.
