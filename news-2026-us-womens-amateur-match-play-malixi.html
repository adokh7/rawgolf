She Won by Six and Five on Thursday. On Friday She Lost on the 19th.

Rianne Malixi beat Jasmine Koo 6 and 5 in the Round of 64 at the U.S. Women's Amateur. Koo was seeded seventh and ranked fourteenth in the world among amateurs. Malixi made four birdies and won by the largest margin of anybody in that round.

It was the second time this summer she'd beaten Koo.

The next match, against Yujie Liu, she went two down after four holes, birdied the sixth and seventh to square it, went two down again through fifteen, then birdied the sixteenth and seventeenth to square it a second time. Nineteen holes. She lost.

That's match play. Nothing carries over. The six-and-five means precisely nothing the following morning.

The Format Club Golfers Actually Play

Almost every serious amateur competition on your calendar is match play — the club championship knockout, the league, the pairs, the Sunday fourball for a fiver. And almost every article about golf is about stroke play, because that's what the professionals do on television.

They aren't the same game and this week at The Honors Course in Ooltewah, Tennessee, showed why.

In stroke play, form is cumulative. A good round banks something. In match play it banks nothing at all — you arrive at the first tee level with whoever is standing there, whether they qualified 64th or you dismantled a top-fifteen player yesterday.

And you can play well and lose. Malixi was two down twice and squared the match twice, both times with back-to-back birdies. Four birdies in the closing stretch of a match she lost. In a medal that's a good afternoon. In match play it's nothing, because Liu also played well and only one of them could go through.

That's the part people find hardest to accept about their own club golf, and it's worth internalising: in match play, playing badly and losing feels the same as playing brilliantly and losing. The scorecard doesn't exist to console you afterwards.

Katie Li's Week

Li shot 71 and 77 for a four-over 148 in stroke play. Malixi shot 77 and 71 — the same total, in the opposite order — and they tied for 51st.

Same number, completely different experience of the two days. One of them was going backwards, the other was arriving.

Li had already reached match play at the New Jersey Women's Amateur last month, where she beat Olivia Gaglione 3 and 2 in the Round of 16, then lost a quarter-final to the top seed by one hole after winning the sixteenth and seventeenth to get back to within one. She's had a summer of tight finishes.

Three Suspensions in Three Days

The other thing worth recording about this championship is the weather.

Play was halted on Wednesday. Halted again Thursday, at 8:43 in the evening for darkness, with three matches still out on the course. And halted on Friday afternoon with all eight Round of 16 matches underway — after nearly three hours of rain and repeated lightning, the USGA called it for the day and sent everyone back out at seven the next morning.

The match-play field itself had been finalised by a nine-for-two playoff after 31 players finished their second stroke-play round that morning. It came down to the sixteenth, a par three over water. One player made bogey and had to stand and watch. Another holed from ten feet. The last one in made a short par putt with her teammate caddying for her.

Nine players, two places, one par three. That's the most match-play thing that can happen before match play has even started.

What to Take to Your Own Club Knockout

Yesterday's result is worth nothing today. Every player in that draw knew it and it still catches people out. If you beat someone comfortably in the last round, you have gained no shots and no strokes, only a reputation that might make your next opponent play better.

Squaring a match is not the same as winning one. Malixi got back to level twice with birdies and lost anyway. Getting back into it is the hard part and it's also only half the job — the danger is the relief that comes with it.

And extra holes are their own game. Two players who have been level for eighteen holes are, by definition, evenly matched. Whatever you were doing to hold the match is now what everyone else is doing too.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is Rianne Malixi?

A Duke sophomore from Manila, Philippines, who set multiple program records as a freshman and is a previous winner of the U.S. Women's Amateur. She reached the Round of 32 at the 2026 championship.

What happened to the Duke players at the 2026 U.S. Women's Amateur?

Both Malixi and Katie Li reached match play at The Honors Course after each shot 148 in stroke play, tying for 51st. Malixi beat seventh seed Jasmine Koo 6 and 5 in the Round of 64 before losing to Yujie Liu on the 19th hole.

Where is the 2026 U.S. Women's Amateur being played?

The Honors Course in Ooltewah, Tennessee. It's the 126th edition of the championship.

Why was play suspended?

Weather interrupted the championship on three consecutive days. Friday's Round of 16 was suspended for the day after roughly three hours of rain and lightning, with all eight matches underway, and resumed on Saturday morning.

How does the U.S. Women's Amateur work?

Two rounds of stroke play determine a 64-player match-play field, followed by knockout rounds, with a 36-hole final on Sunday.
