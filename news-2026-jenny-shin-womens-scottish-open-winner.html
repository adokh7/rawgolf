Jenny Shin Wins Scottish Open After 3,738 Days

BY GOLFRAW EDITORIAL PUBLISHED SUN 26 JUL 2026

The Quick Verdict

Jenny Shin won the 2026 ISPS Handa Women's Scottish Open on Sunday, 26 July, at Dundonald Links in North Ayrshire, Scotland. She finished at 9-under 279 after a closing 3-over 75, beating A Lim Kim by two shots. It was her second LPGA Tour win and her first since the 2016 Volunteers of America Texas Shootout — a gap of 3,738 days. Do the math on that number and it stops being trivia. Ten years and change. Shin was 23 the last time she held a trophy. She's 33 now.

How close did she come to throwing it away?

Closer than the two-shot margin suggests.

Shin took a five-stroke lead into Sunday and pushed it to eight after two holes. At that point this was a coronation, not a golf tournament. Then Dundonald did what links courses do to people who are thinking about the trophy.

Bogeys at 6, 7 and 8. Three in a row, the lead shrinking with every one. She stopped the slide with birdies at 10 and 12, then bogeyed 16 and 18 anyway. She tapped in for bogey on the last to win.

Her own read on it: "I feel like I've done everything wrong, just wasn't hitting it like I was the last three days and was hitting it into bunkers, bushes, and it wasn't fun." On the three-bogey stretch, she said she genuinely didn't know where the round was heading.

A 75 to win a golf tournament. That's not a highlight reel. That's survival, and it's the more interesting story.

What did the rest of the leaderboard do?

A Lim Kim shot 68 to climb into second at 7-under, two clear of Thailand's Pajaree Anannarukarn, who closed with a 74. Kim's move came off four straight birdies. If Shin had leaked one more shot anywhere on that back nine, this is a different article.

The bigger names never got going. The wind at Dundonald had already sorted them out earlier in the week — world No. 1 Nelly Korda shot 76 in round two to fall to 4-over, including four consecutive bogeys to close, and Charley Hull needed four chips to escape a hole en route to a quadruple-bogey 9 in round one. Defending champion Lottie Woad struggled with the putter through the weekend.

One correction worth flagging, since a few preview pieces got it wrong: Woad was the defending champion here, having won at Dundonald in 2025 in her professional debut. Lauren Coughlin won the 2024 edition.

Why does the 3,738-day number matter?

Because most players in that situation don't come back. They lose their card, or they grind out top-25s, or they quietly move on. Shin kept a tour job for a decade without winning, which is its own kind of hard.

She's also been explicit that this one lands differently than the first: "My first win didn't really feel like a win at the time. I didn't feel like I deserved it. But this one definitely feels a lot different. This one feels like I earned it." She credited work started with a putting coach four years ago.

There's a small technical detail that's easy to miss. Earlier in the week she mentioned standing taller at address to feel more stable in the wind — not a rebuild, just one adjustment for one week's conditions. Whether that's the reason she won is unprovable. But it's the kind of thing that separates a player who's guessing from a player who's actually working on something.

What happens next?

The Women's British Open starts Thursday at Royal Lytham & St Annes. The Scottish is part of a European swing sitting between the season's final two majors, the Evian Championship and the Women's Open.

Dundonald was a genuine links test this year — firm, windy, and it wrecked several top-10 players. Lytham won't be identical, but anyone who handled the ground game this week just banked something useful.

GOLFRAW's Take

Everybody's going to write the fairytale version of this, and fine, it is one. But the part worth stealing if you're a normal golfer is the 75. Shin played badly on Sunday. She admitted it before anyone could ask. She hit it in bunkers and bushes, made three bogeys in a row with the whole thing on the line, and did not go into the ditch. Most weekend rounds fall apart at exactly that point — not because of the bad holes, but because of what you do in the four holes after them. She birdied 10 and 12. That's the whole lesson, and it cost her nothing but patience.
