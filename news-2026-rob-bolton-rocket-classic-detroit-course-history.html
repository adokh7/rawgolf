The PGA Tour's Own Analyst Just Threw Out Detroit's Course History

The Tour's own fantasy analyst says all 147 players start at zero this week. Here's why, the stat that proves it, and the correction we owe you.

BY GOLFRAW EDITORIAL PUBLISHED: WED 29 JUL 2026
The Quick Verdict

In his Fantasy Insider column published Tuesday, PGA TOUR analyst Rob Bolton wrote that he "ignored all course history" when compiling his Rocket Classic Power Rankings — with one passing exception for Rickie Fowler as the 2023 champion.

His reasoning: Detroit Golf Club has been rebuilt from a par 72 into a par 70, and all 147 players in the field this week are starting from zero. Any golfer who has played well here before and plays well again, he argues, will be a coincidence rather than a course fit.

He also supplies the statistic that explains why the old reputation was so strong: as a par 72 in 2025, Detroit Golf Club yielded more par breakers than any course on the PGA TOUR.

Why this matters more than a fantasy column usually does

We published a piece yesterday arguing that every preview of this tournament was using dead data — that the birdie-fest reputation belonged to a golf course that no longer exists, and that the course-fit models everyone was quoting had been trained on a layout the diggers removed.

That argument now has the Tour's own analyst behind it, arriving at it independently and stating it more bluntly than we did.

One honest caveat. Bolton wrote both the Power Rankings and the Fantasy Insider column, so this is one analyst saying it twice, plus our own reasoning from the par change. It isn't a chorus. It's two lines of thinking that happen to agree, and they agree because the underlying fact — two shots of par gone — is not really arguable.

The number we didn't have

Here's what Bolton adds that we couldn't.

In 2025, Detroit Golf Club produced more par breakers than any other course on Tour. Not one of the easiest. The easiest, by that measure.

That's the quantitative backing for seven years of "back the bombers, it's a birdie-fest" advice. The advice wasn't lazy. It was correct — about a golf course that has since been converted to a par 70 at 7,328 yards after a $16 million Tyler Rae restoration, and reconfigured into a composite layout that borrows a hole from the club's South Course.

Worth flagging: that statistic comes from the PGA TOUR, which owns ShotLink and can compute it, and it's published without a supporting figure. Nobody outside the Tour can check it. It's almost certainly right. It's still their number.

The correction we owe you

Yesterday we said the field was "around 145," following the tournament's own website.

Bolton says 147. He's the PGA TOUR, and he's the most recent, so go with 147.

For the record, in the space of four days the field has been published as 144, 145, 147 and "more than 150." That's normal — commitments and withdrawals move until they don't — but if you're building anything that depends on the number, check it Thursday morning rather than trusting any preview, including this one.

The bit that frames the whole week

Bolton makes one more point that nobody else has, and it's the most useful sentence in the column.

Next week is the Wyndham Championship, where course history will be a primary driver.

So this is a one-week suspension, not a new era. Everything you normally lean on returns in seven days. Which means the sensible read isn't "course fit is dead" — it's "course fit is unavailable at this specific tournament, for a specific and temporary reason, and you should get your comfort from somewhere else for four rounds."

His own suggestion is to use the blank slate to lock in the players you actually want anchoring your Playoff rosters, rather than chasing a fit that doesn't exist.

What he's actually forecasting

Bolton's expectation is that the rebuilt par 70 will still be friendly.

That's a prediction, not a finding, and it's worth separating from everything above. The par change is a fact. The par-breaker statistic is a record of the past. Whether the new layout still gives up birdies is unknown to him, to us, and to the 147 players teeing off on Thursday — because no professional tournament has ever been played on it.

He may well be right. Rae's restorations aren't generally about brutalising a golf course. But it's a guess with a good pedigree, not evidence.

The ownership numbers, and why to hold them loosely

The column publishes participation data from PGA TOUR Fantasy Golf. A few that stood out:

Cameron Young is comfortably the most-owned player in the field, at roughly 64%, despite sitting sixth in Bolton's own rankings — a gap between market and analyst that's interesting in itself.

Jackson Koivun has gone from 9.7% to 37% in a week, after winning the 3M Open. That's what a maiden victory does.

Aldrich Potgieter, the defending champion, is on 3.4% of rosters. Bolton flags this as lower than he expected, and wonders whether it reflects an inconsistent season or the fact that the course he won on has been rebuilt.

Two caveats, both important. These figures come from a game only the PGA TOUR can see, so nobody can audit them. And they move continuously until lock — Bolton time-stamps his own as "at last check," which is the right way to publish them and the detail most aggregation will strip out.

One inference of our own, clearly labelled as ours: Bolton notes Koivun was 16th-most-owned at the 3M Open on 9.7% of rosters. If you can be 16th out of 147 while appearing on fewer than one roster in ten, the ownership curve in that format is extremely flat below the top handful. That's a structural point about the game, and Bolton doesn't make it — we're reading it off his two numbers.

The human bit

Buried at the bottom of a fantasy column is the sort of thing that deserves its own piece.

Patton Kizzire withdrew from the 3M Open after opening with a 77, with a sore back. He's 4-for-16 this season and 162nd in the FedExCup. He's 40. And the membership exemption he earned by winning the 2024 Procore Championship expires this season.

Bolton's advice is to avoid him in all formats, which is correct and also the coldest possible framing of a career at a hinge point.

FAQ

Does Detroit Golf Club course history matter this week?

No. The PGA TOUR's own analyst ignored it entirely, on the basis that the renovated course makes all prior data irrelevant.

Why not?

It's been rebuilt from a par 72 into a par 70 at 7,328 yards, with a composite routing that uses a hole from the club's South Course.

How easy was the old course?

Per Bolton, as a par 72 in 2025 it yielded more par breakers than any course on the PGA TOUR.

How big is the field?

147, per the Tour. Note that 144, 145 and "more than 150" have all been published in the last four days.

Who does Bolton rank first?

Si Woo Kim, with Chris Gotterup second. Cameron Young is sixth. Those are his opinions and his rankings — read them at the source.

Who is most-owned in PGA TOUR Fantasy Golf?

Cameron Young, at roughly 64% at his last check.

Is the new course still going to be easy?

He expects so. Nobody knows — no professional round has been played on it.

Does course history matter next week?

Yes. He explicitly names the Wyndham Championship as a venue where it will be a primary driver.

GOLFRAW's Take

We wrote yesterday that everybody's Rocket Classic analysis was built on a golf course that got bulldozed, and it's a slightly odd feeling to be handed the receipt by the PGA TOUR the following afternoon. But the useful part isn't that we were right. It's the number. The most birdie-friendly course on Tour in 2025 is exactly the reputation that made all the old advice sensible, and it's exactly the reputation that just stopped applying. Anyone still telling you to back bombers because Detroit gives up birdies is quoting a stat about a course that was demolished. And we got the field size wrong, which is the smaller lesson: if you're going to spend articles checking other people's numbers, you check your own in public when they turn out to be off by two. One last thing. Everything in Bolton's column that's a fact — the par, the par-breakers, the field — comes from the PGA TOUR. Everything that's a pick is his judgement. Everything about ownership is from a game only his employer can see. That's not a criticism; it's the best-sourced fantasy column in golf. It just means the three categories deserve three different levels of trust, and columns like his get aggregated into pages that flatten all three into "the PGA Tour says."
